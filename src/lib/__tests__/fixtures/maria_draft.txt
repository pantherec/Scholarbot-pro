I was eleven years old the first time a doctor asked me to explain to my grandmother that her blood pressure medication needed to change. I remember standing between them, trying to find the right Spanish words for dosage and side effects, watching her face for any sign that she understood. She nodded, but I wasn't sure if she really did. That afternoon stayed with me. I realized that good care depends on more than what a doctor knows. It depends on whether the patient actually understands what is happening to their own body.

That memory is why I started volunteering at the hospital near my house in Rochester as a Candy Striper. Over three years I've logged more than 200 hours, and a lot of that time has been spent doing exactly what I did for my grandmother: translating for Spanish-speaking patients who are scared, confused, or just tired of nodding along without understanding. I've sat with families in waiting rooms and repeated instructions slowly, in both languages, until I could see relief on their faces instead of confusion.

The hardest part was learning the vocabulary. Medical Spanish is not the Spanish I grew up speaking at home, and I didn't want to be the reason someone misunderstood their own diagnosis. I started studying medical terms on my own time, asking nurses to walk me through words I didn't know, and keeping a small notebook of terms I'd picked up. Spanish Honor Society helped too, but most of what I know now came from showing up, again and again, until the words stopped feeling foreign.

As HOSA Vice President at Wilson Magnet, I've helped organize our club's meetings and prepare members for regional competition, where I earned a Silver Medal. Leading that group taught me that leadership in healthcare isn't about having all the answers. It's about making sure everyone in the room, whether it's a club member or a patient's family, has what they need to participate fully.

I'm planning to study nursing and become a bilingual pediatric nurse working with underinsured families, the kind of families who look like mine. I know what it feels like to sit in an exam room and not fully understand what's being said, and I know what it feels like when someone finally explains it in a way that makes sense. I want to be that person for other families, in Rochester and wherever I end up practicing.

The first time I taught a coding workshop at the community center in Rochester, only four middle schoolers showed up. One of them, a sixth grader who kept erasing his code because he was afraid of getting it wrong, spent twenty minutes stuck on a single loop. I sat next to him and we rebuilt it together, line by line, until it ran. By the end of that semester, the workshop had grown enough that I needed a waitlist.

I started that workshop because I remembered what it felt like to not understand the rules of a new place. Before I turned ten, I moved between two countries, and each move meant learning a new school system, a new way of speaking, sometimes a new alphabet of unspoken expectations. I got good at reading a room fast and figuring out what I was missing. That skill followed me into computer science, where I now think about software less as a set of commands and more as something that has to adjust to the person using it. A program that assumes everyone starts from the same place will fail the kids I taught at the community center just as surely as a new country failed to explain itself to me.

That same instinct shaped how I built the NSBE chapter at School of the Arts. I did not inherit an organization. I started with a list of names, a classroom borrowed on Thursday afternoons, and a stack of flyers I made myself. Debate Team gave me practice turning a half formed argument into something a judge could follow, and Math Olympiad taught me to sit with a problem long after the easy approaches stopped working. None of that happened in isolation. I carried the patience from one into the other.

My status under DACA and TPS has meant that some plans I make have to bend around paperwork and timing I cannot control. I have learned to keep building anyway, because the workshop still needs to happen on Tuesday whether or not everything else in my life feels settled.

I want to become a machine learning engineer who builds accessible technology, tools that work for someone using a screen reader, or a translation app that does not flatten a person's first language into something clumsy. The sixth grader who was afraid to run his code eventually taught the lesson himself, to a newer student, last spring. I think about that a lot. I want my career to keep creating moments like that one, where someone learns they can build something that did not exist before, and then hands that confidence to the next person.
